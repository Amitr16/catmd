# Cat Eye Discharge: URI, Corneal Ulcer, or Herpesvirus?

Eye discharge in a cat is almost never just "a bit goopy." It's almost always one of three clinical conditions — each with different urgency, treatment, and long-term implications. Getting the right one matters because the wrong treatment can make feline eye disease much worse.

## The three common culprits

### 1. Feline upper respiratory infection (URI)

**Presentation:** watery then mucopurulent (yellow/green) discharge from one or both eyes, paired with nasal discharge, sneezing, reduced appetite, possibly fever. Cats often squint.

**Cause:** most commonly **feline herpesvirus (FHV-1)** or **calicivirus**, sometimes bacterial (Chlamydophila, Mycoplasma, Bordetella).

**Urgency:** vet visit within 24-48 hours. Supportive care plus antiviral/antibiotic eye drops.

**Key gotcha:** if the eye is squinted shut, an ulcer may be developing (see next section).

### 2. Corneal ulcer

**Presentation:** one eye, squinting shut (sometimes almost completely), excessive tearing, visible haze on the cornea, pain (cat rubs the eye or avoids light). No sneezing.

**Cause:** trauma (cat scratch, plant matter, self-rubbing), FHV-1 reactivation, dry eye, or secondary bacterial infection.

**Urgency:** same-day vet visit. Corneal ulcers can deepen rapidly and lead to perforation.

**Critical warning:** **DO NOT use any eye drops containing steroids on a cat with an ulcer.** Steroids accelerate ulcer deepening and can lead to eye loss. If you have old steroid drops from another cat, do not use them. Only vet-prescribed drops after evaluation.

### 3. Feline herpesvirus flare (FHV-1)

**Presentation:** typically one eye, chronic or recurrent, mild-to-moderate discharge, squinting, often recurs under stress or immunosuppression. Most cats are exposed in kittenhood and carry the virus for life. Adults show flares during stress.

**Urgency:** vet visit within a few days for diagnosis and antiviral therapy. Chronic cases benefit from lysine supplementation and stress reduction.

**Key detail:** FHV-1 can cause **dendritic corneal ulcers** with a characteristic branching shape — visible only with fluorescein stain at the vet.

## Quick differentiation guide

| Sign | URI | Corneal ulcer | FHV-1 flare |
|---|---|---|---|
| One or both eyes | Often both | One | One (usually) |
| Sneezing | Yes | No | Sometimes |
| Squinting severity | Mild to moderate | Severe | Moderate |
| Discharge character | Yellow/green mucopurulent | Often watery | Watery to yellow |
| Pain level | Moderate | High | Moderate |
| Recurrence | Acute | One event | Chronic/recurrent |
| Urgency | 24-48h vet | Same-day vet | Few-days vet |

## The less-common but don't-miss causes

- **Glaucoma** — enlarged eye, severe pain, vision loss. Emergency.
- **Uveitis** (intraocular inflammation) — often secondary to FIP, FeLV, FIV, toxoplasmosis. Cloudy eye, pain. Vet visit.
- **Foreign body** (grass, dust, cat claw debris) — severe squinting, usually one eye. Same-day vet.
- **Tear duct blockage** — chronic watery discharge from inner corner. Brachycephalic breeds (Persians, Himalayans) prone.
- **Entropion** (inward-rolled eyelid) — chronic irritation. Mostly breed-specific.
- **Eyelid tumor** — older cats, visible lump on eyelid.

## Red flags — emergency vet

Any of these: same-day emergency evaluation.

- Eye visibly bulging or enlarged
- Eye looks sunken or smaller than the other
- Significant discharge of blood from the eye
- Cat won't open the eye at all, and it's getting worse by the hour
- Visible foreign object on or in the eye
- Pupil fixed (non-responsive to light change) in one eye
- Trauma to head or face
- Kitten with both eyes gummed shut and not eating

## What you can do at home (while waiting for the vet)

- Gently wipe discharge with a warm, damp cotton pad
- Use a separate pad per eye (prevents cross-contamination)
- Do NOT use any human eye drops or saline contact solutions
- Do NOT flush with hydrogen peroxide or alcohol
- Keep other pets separate (herpesvirus and chlamydia are contagious between cats)
- Reduce stress (stress reactivates herpesvirus)

## After diagnosis: typical treatments

- **Bacterial conjunctivitis:** topical antibiotic drops (tobramycin, ciprofloxacin)
- **FHV-1:** antiviral drops (idoxuridine or cidofovir), oral antiviral (famciclovir), lysine supplementation
- **Corneal ulcer:** atropine for pain, topical antibiotic, possibly autologous serum drops, rechecks every few days
- **Chlamydophila:** oral doxycycline + topical antibiotics

## Post-treatment monitoring

Most feline eye infections resolve within 7-10 days with correct treatment. If your cat's eye is not visibly better after 3-5 days of prescribed treatment, call the vet — you may need a different drug or a secondary infection may have developed.

FHV-1 positive cats will have recurrent flares during stress. Add enrichment (cat trees, multiple litter boxes, hiding spots) and consider lysine supplementation.
