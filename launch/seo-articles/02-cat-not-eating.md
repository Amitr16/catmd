# Cat Not Eating: Causes, Timeline, and Red Flags

A cat that stops eating is not just being picky. Unlike dogs, cats develop a specific and dangerous complication called **hepatic lipidosis** (fatty liver disease) when they don't eat — sometimes in as little as 48-72 hours. This is why "he'll eat when he's hungry enough" is dangerous advice for cats.

Here's the clear, cat-specific guide to what appetite loss means, when it becomes an emergency, and what to do before you call the vet.

## The timeline that matters

| Duration without eating | What's happening | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| **0-12 hours** | Could be normal (stress, mild GI upset, finicky) | Monitor; offer favorite food |
| **12-24 hours** | Starting to matter, especially if also vomiting | Vet call/message recommended |
| **24-48 hours** | Risk of hepatic lipidosis starts to rise (especially in overweight cats) | **Vet visit within 24h** |
| **48-72 hours** | Hepatic lipidosis risk real and rising | **Urgent vet visit** |
| **72+ hours** | Medical emergency | **Emergency vet** |

Overweight cats are at highest risk. A fat cat that stops eating is a medical emergency faster than a lean one.

## Why cats can't "just skip meals" the way dogs can

When a cat stops eating, their body starts mobilizing body fat for energy. But cats' livers can't process fat efficiently — fat accumulates inside liver cells, causing **hepatic lipidosis**. Once this starts, the cat feels even worse, eats less, more fat mobilizes, and you have a spiral that ends in liver failure.

Hepatic lipidosis is **the most common liver disease in cats**, and its trigger is almost always "cat stopped eating for another reason and it snowballed." It's preventable with early intervention. It's fatal without.

This is why feline appetite loss is almost always a vet visit — regardless of the underlying cause.

## The common causes (ordered by frequency)

### 1. Dental pain
Resorptive lesions, gingivitis, fractured teeth. Cats hide dental pain extremely well; often the first visible sign is they stop eating dry food or drop pieces of food from their mouth. Your vet can often diagnose on exam.

### 2. Stress or environmental change
New person, new pet, moved house, new furniture, litter box moved, dog staying over. Some cats are very stress-sensitive about their eating environment. Give it 24 hours, then call the vet if not improving.

### 3. Upper respiratory infection (URI)
Cats who can't smell don't eat. A congested cat loses appetite within a day. Look for sneezing, nasal discharge, squinting eyes.

### 4. Gastrointestinal disease
Nausea from inflammatory bowel disease, chronic pancreatitis, or food intolerance. Often paired with vomiting or diarrhea.

### 5. Kidney disease (especially senior cats)
CKD causes nausea that worsens in the afternoon/evening. A senior cat who eats breakfast but refuses dinner fits this pattern. Weight loss, increased water intake, and vomiting often co-present.

### 6. Hyperthyroidism (senior cats, atypical presentation)
Classic hyperthyroidism causes *increased* appetite. But end-stage or atypical thyroid disease can flip to anorexia.

### 7. Liver disease (including the hepatic lipidosis that anorexia itself causes)
Yellow gums, vomiting, weight loss over weeks.

### 8. Urethral obstruction (male cats)
A blocked cat stops eating because of systemic metabolic effects. Look for straining in the litter box with no urine output. **Emergency.**

### 9. Foreign body obstruction
Swallowed string, thread, ribbon, hair tie, small toy. Vomiting with anorexia.

### 10. Cancer
Lymphoma, intestinal, oral. Less common than 1-9 but on the list for any cat with weeks of progressive appetite decline.

## Red flags — emergency now

Any of these and go to a vet immediately:

- Cat hasn't eaten for 72+ hours
- Overweight cat who hasn't eaten for 48+ hours
- Appetite loss + yellow gums or yellow skin (jaundice = liver failure)
- Appetite loss + vomiting + hiding
- Appetite loss + straining to urinate (males especially)
- Appetite loss + fast or labored breathing
- Appetite loss + collapse or unresponsiveness
- Appetite loss + visible string or thread hanging from mouth — **do not pull**

## Things to try at home (for the first 12-24 hours only)

Before assuming it's medical, rule out pickiness:

- Warm the food to body temperature (releases smell)
- Offer a **strongly-scented** favorite — tuna juice, a tiny piece of cooked chicken, kitten food
- Move the dish away from water or litter (cats dislike contamination)
- Try a different bowl (shallow, wide — not deep)
- Offer in a quiet, low-traffic area
- Let them eat from your hand if they'll take it

If none of this works within 24 hours, stop trying and call the vet. You're not wasting their time.

## What to document for the vet

- How long the cat has been off food (exact last meal date/time)
- Any vomiting, diarrhea, constipation
- Water intake — increased, decreased, normal
- Urination frequency and effort
- Weight change in the last 1-3 months
- Any new foods, medications, supplements
- Access to plants, human food, trash, strings
- Recent stress events
