# Cat Losing Weight: CKD, Hyperthyroidism, and the Three Differentials You Need to Know

If your senior cat is losing weight — even slowly, even while eating well — please read this. Unintentional weight loss in cats is one of the earliest and most important signs of serious disease, and the three biggest causes are all eminently treatable *if* caught before weight loss becomes dramatic.

## The rule of thumb

**A cat losing more than 5% of body weight in a month needs a vet visit.** For a 4 kg (9 lb) cat, that's just 200 g. Easy to miss at home; your vet will catch it on the scale.

**Any cat over 10 years old who is losing weight needs a vet visit regardless of appetite.** Senior cats do not "just get skinny." They develop diseases.

## The three big differentials

### 1. Hyperthyroidism

**Classic presentation:** older cat (10+ years), losing weight *despite an increased appetite*, often vocal/restless, sometimes vomiting or drinking more than usual. May have patchy coat or dull fur.

**What it is:** a benign tumor on the thyroid gland over-produces thyroid hormone. The cat's metabolism runs too hot. They burn weight even while eating more.

**How vets diagnose:** a simple blood test (T4, often total T4 + free T4 if borderline). Often screened as part of a senior wellness panel.

**Treatment:** very effective. Options include daily oral medication (methimazole), a prescription iodine-restricted diet (Hill's y/d), radioactive iodine therapy (curative, gold standard), or surgery.

**Prognosis:** excellent if treated early.

### 2. Chronic kidney disease (CKD)

**Classic presentation:** older cat, gradual weight loss over months, increased thirst and urination, reduced appetite (especially in the evening), occasional vomiting, dull coat, bad breath with an ammonia-like smell.

**What it is:** the kidneys are progressively losing function. By the time symptoms appear, 70%+ of kidney function is already lost.

**How vets diagnose:** blood chemistry (creatinine, SDMA, BUN, phosphorus), urinalysis (urine specific gravity, protein, sediment). SDMA catches earlier than creatinine.

**Treatment:** not curable, but progression is dramatically slowed with early intervention. Tools include: prescription renal diets, subcutaneous fluids, phosphate binders, blood pressure medication, anti-nausea medications, and increasingly, telmisartan for proteinuria.

**Prognosis:** Cats diagnosed in IRIS stage 2 and treated can live 2-4+ additional years. Diagnosed late, months.

### 3. Diabetes mellitus

**Classic presentation:** overweight middle-aged cat who starts losing weight while drinking and urinating more. Appetite often increased initially, then drops. Weakness in hind legs (diabetic neuropathy) in advanced cases.

**What it is:** pancreas fails to produce enough insulin, or cells become resistant. Blood sugar rises; cat pees it out, drinks more to compensate, and the cells starve despite adequate food.

**How vets diagnose:** blood glucose + fructosamine (long-term glucose marker, since cats get stress hyperglycemia at the vet). Urinalysis shows glucose.

**Treatment:** insulin injections (usually Lantus or ProZinc), low-carbohydrate diet, weight management. A significant fraction of cats go into diabetic remission within 6 months if caught early and treated aggressively.

**Prognosis:** very good with early treatment; diabetic ketoacidosis is a life-threatening complication of untreated diabetes.

## The less-common (but important) differentials

- **Inflammatory bowel disease (IBD)** — gradual weight loss, intermittent vomiting or diarrhea
- **Intestinal lymphoma** — often presents like IBD but worsens steadily
- **Chronic pancreatitis** — episodic vomiting, appetite fluctuation
- **Dental pain** — cat eats less because chewing hurts
- **Heart disease** — weight loss in end-stage HCM
- **Cancer** — multiple sites, progressive weight loss
- **Hyperadrenocorticism** (rare in cats)
- **Chronic infection** (FIV, FeLV, dental abscess)

## Why "he's just getting old" is wrong

"Cat cachexia" — muscle wasting in old age — is not a diagnosis. It's a symptom. Something is causing it. The above list represents 90%+ of causes, and most are treatable.

A senior cat losing weight should have, at minimum, this lab panel:

- CBC (complete blood count)
- Full chemistry panel (including SDMA for early kidney detection)
- T4 (thyroid)
- Urinalysis
- Ideally: blood pressure measurement (related to CKD and hyperthyroidism)

This costs around $150-$300 and can diagnose 3 of the 4 biggest causes.

## What to document before the vet visit

- Weight history — track for a month before the visit if possible
- Appetite trend (eating more, same, or less)
- Water intake (normal, increased, or decreased — measure with a measuring cup if you can)
- Litter box output (more urine, less urine, diarrhea, constipation)
- Activity level (more restless, less active)
- Coat quality
- Any vomiting frequency/type
- New behaviors (vocalizing at night, hiding, aggression)

## Tracking weight at home

Weigh your cat monthly. The easiest method: weigh yourself on a bathroom scale, then weigh yourself holding the cat. Subtract. Record in a journal or app.

A baby scale is more accurate if you have one. Even a 100-200g loss matters for a small cat.

## Red flags — urgent vet visit

- Weight loss + not eating
- Weight loss + vomiting blood or coffee-ground material
- Weight loss + hiding + lethargy
- Weight loss + rapid breathing (possible heart disease)
- Weight loss + jaundice (yellow gums)
- Weight loss + a palpable lump or mass
- Any weight loss in a diabetic cat (ketoacidosis risk)
- Weight loss + collapse, weakness, or inability to stand
