# Cat Gum Color: Pink, Pale, Yellow, or Blue — What Each Means

Gum color is the single fastest, cheapest home check of a cat's cardiovascular and metabolic status. Vets assess it every exam. You can too. It takes 3 seconds and can tell you whether something is a "call tomorrow" situation or a "go to the ER right now" situation.

## How to check

Lift your cat's upper lip gently and look at the gum tissue above the canine tooth. In a healthy cat, it should be **bubblegum pink** — a clean, uniform pink color. Press lightly with your fingertip; the spot you pressed should turn white, then pink again in **under 2 seconds** (this is called capillary refill time, or CRT).

Some cats have naturally pigmented gums (black or mottled), especially Bombay, Siamese, and some mixed-breed cats. Find a non-pigmented area — the tongue underside works if gums are all dark.

## The color chart

### Healthy pink (normal)
Uniform light-to-bubblegum pink, CRT under 2 seconds. Cat is fine from a circulatory standpoint.

### Pale pink or white (anemia / shock / blood loss)
**Emergency.** Causes:
- Internal bleeding (trauma, toxin-induced — e.g., rat poison)
- Anemia (parasite burden in kittens, chronic kidney disease, autoimmune, cancer)
- Severe dehydration
- Shock (any cause — trauma, sepsis, cardiac)
- Hypothermia

**What to do:** go to the emergency vet immediately. Pale gums + weakness = circulatory collapse.

### Yellow / jaundiced (icterus)
**Emergency.** Causes:
- Hepatic lipidosis (fatty liver disease)
- Cholangitis (liver/bile duct infection)
- Hemolysis (red blood cell destruction — can be autoimmune, toxin, or infection)
- Liver tumor
- Extrahepatic biliary obstruction

**What to do:** same-day vet visit. Yellow gums mean the liver isn't processing bilirubin, or red blood cells are being destroyed. Both need prompt workup.

### Bright red (shock / sepsis / toxin / overheating)
**Emergency.** Causes:
- Septic shock
- Heat stroke
- Carbon monoxide poisoning
- Severe allergic reaction
- Toxin exposure (sometimes paradoxically red early, then pale)

**What to do:** emergency vet. Also check body temperature — overheating is likely if ambient was hot.

### Blue / purple (cyanosis)
**Emergency.** Causes:
- Severe respiratory distress
- Heart failure
- Pleural effusion (fluid around lungs)
- Airway obstruction
- Cyanide or severe oxygen deprivation

**What to do:** blue gums + any breathing difficulty = call ahead to the ER vet, carry the cat to the car, drive. Do not wait.

### Tacky / dry (dehydration)
Press your finger firmly; a normal cat's gum is moist and wet. Dry, tacky gums mean dehydration — which in a cat is usually a few percent body water lost. Combined with skin tenting (scruff skin doesn't snap back in 1 second), it's a vet visit.

## Using CRT (capillary refill time) to sharpen the signal

Press your fingertip firmly against the gum for 1 second, then release. Count how many seconds it takes for the pressed-white spot to return to its original pink.

- **Under 1 second:** may indicate hyperdynamic circulation (early shock, excitement)
- **1-2 seconds:** normal
- **2-3 seconds:** mild dehydration or poor perfusion — vet visit
- **Over 3 seconds:** significant circulatory compromise — emergency

## Combine gum color with two other fast checks

### 1. Heart rate
Place your palm flat on your cat's chest behind the elbow. Count beats for 15 seconds and multiply by 4.
- Normal: 140-220 bpm (cats have fast hearts; higher than dogs)
- Under 100: concerning
- Over 240 at rest: concerning

### 2. Respiratory rate (at rest or asleep)
Count chest rises for 30 seconds and multiply by 2.
- Normal awake: 20-30/min
- Normal asleep: under 30/min
- Over 40 asleep: abnormal — vet call

**The three together** — gum color, heart rate, respiratory rate — catch roughly 90% of cats in circulatory or respiratory crisis within 60 seconds.

## When to run these checks

- Any time your cat seems "off"
- After a fall, scuffle, or suspected trauma
- If your cat has been exposed to anything potentially toxic
- Senior cats with CKD or heart disease — monthly at minimum
- Any cat refusing food or hiding

## What NOT to do

- Do not give aspirin, Tylenol, or ibuprofen to a cat under any circumstance. These are fatal in cats, even in doses tolerable to dogs.
- Do not force your finger deep into the mouth if the cat is distressed.
- Do not delay based on "maybe their gums are just naturally pale."
