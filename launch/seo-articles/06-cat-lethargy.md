# Cat Lethargy: What It Means at Different Ages

Cats sleep 12-16 hours a day. That's normal. What's not normal is a cat who stops responding to their usual triggers — food bowl, toy, owner returning home, a bird at the window — and just lies there. That's not tiredness. That's lethargy. And in cats, lethargy is usually a medical sign, not a mood.

## Distinguishing "normal cat energy" from lethargy

Ask yourself:

- Does she come when called for dinner? (Normally yes; now no = concerning)
- Does he jump on his favorite perch? (Normally yes; now skipping = concerning)
- Does she greet you at the door? (Normally yes; now hiding = concerning)
- Is he grooming himself? (Cats who feel unwell often stop grooming)
- Is the sleep posture normal, or hunched/head-low/paws-tucked? (Hunched posture = discomfort)

A cat who's "just tired" still responds to the food bowl, still blinks slowly when you make eye contact, still grooms. A lethargic cat is absent in a way that's qualitatively different.

## Causes by age

### Kitten (under 1 year)

Kittens have very little physiologic reserve. Lethargy in a kitten is a fast-moving medical situation.

- **Panleukopenia (feline parvovirus)** — deadly without fast treatment
- **URI (upper respiratory infection)** — congested kittens stop eating and crash fast
- **Parasites** (roundworms, hookworms) causing anemia
- **Hypoglycemia** — kittens can crash from low blood sugar within hours
- **Congenital heart defect** becoming symptomatic
- **Toxin ingestion** — lily, antifreeze, human medications, essential oils

**Rule:** any kitten who is lethargic and not eating for 12+ hours needs a vet. Under 12+ hours, watch closely but call a vet for advice.

### Young/adult cat (1-7 years)

- **Urethral obstruction (males)** — lethargy appears as metabolic crisis progresses
- **FIC (feline idiopathic cystitis)** — painful urinary condition
- **Upper respiratory infection**
- **Trauma** — cat fights, falls, being hit (especially indoor/outdoor cats)
- **Pancreatitis** — vague presentation, often just lethargy + anorexia
- **Toxin ingestion**
- **Systemic infection** (abscess, bite wound, infected uterus in unspayed females)
- **Heart disease** (HCM presenting)
- **FIP (feline infectious peritonitis)** — historically fatal, now treatable with GS-441524

### Senior cat (10+ years)

- **Chronic kidney disease**
- **Hyperthyroidism**
- **Diabetes mellitus** (especially with DKA — diabetic ketoacidosis)
- **Hypertension** (often secondary to CKD or thyroid)
- **Heart disease** (HCM, DCM, arrhythmias)
- **Cancer** (lymphoma, various)
- **Hepatic lipidosis** (secondary to any disease that causes anorexia)
- **Arthritis** — not fatal but causes chronic low energy

## The red-flag combinations

Lethargy + any of the following is always "emergency vet now":

- Pale, yellow, or blue gums
- Fast or labored breathing
- Collapse or inability to stand
- Hind leg weakness (possible arterial thromboembolism — immediate emergency)
- Straining in litter box (urinating or defecating)
- Vomiting or diarrhea that's blood-tinged
- Fever that's detectable (cat's body feels hot to touch)
- Very low body temperature (cold ears, cold paws, hunched)
- Seizure or disorientation
- Bloat, distended abdomen

## The FATE score (veterinary triage heuristic)

A simple bedside assessment your vet uses: **F**luid loss, **A**ppetite, **T**emperature, **E**nergy. If three of four are abnormal, it's an urgent visit.

You can run this yourself:
- **F** — dehydration: lift skin at scruff; if it doesn't snap back in under 1 second, they're dehydrated
- **A** — eating/drinking normally?
- **T** — body temperature (100.5-102.5°F / 38-39.2°C normal)
- **E** — responding to usual stimuli?

## What to document

Before you call the vet:

- When did the lethargy start (hours/days)?
- What's different today vs. yesterday?
- Eating and drinking status
- Litter box activity (normal, straining, diarrhea, no output)
- Vomiting (number of times, appearance)
- Any new medications, foods, plants, or household changes
- Any access to outdoors / other pets in last 2-3 days
- Recent trauma opportunity (falls, fights)
- Vaccination status

## The home temperature trick

A normal cat's ear tips are warm. A lethargic cat with cold ears and cold paws is often in circulatory shock — don't wait, go to the vet. A cat with very warm ears and panting may have a fever.

A rectal thermometer is the only accurate way to measure. If you can:

- Lubricate with petroleum jelly
- Insert ½-1 inch
- Wait 30-60 seconds (digital)
- Normal: 100.5-102.5°F (38-39.2°C)
- Below 99°F (37.2°C): hypothermia — emergency
- Above 103.5°F (39.7°C): fever — vet soon
- Above 104°F (40°C): high fever — emergency
